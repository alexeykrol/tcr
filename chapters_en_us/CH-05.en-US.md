PART 3. PERSONALITY AND ITS ROLES

How the Structure of Personality Influences Our Fate
From the previous part we learned that the world is a pyramid. Each of its levels is a caste that creates certain conditions for its members: growth, freedom of self-expression, accumulation of wealth, and control of property. In the lower castes a person's opportunities are minimal; in the upper castes they are maximal.

If a person strives to fully realize their potential, they will have to organize their life in such a way that they move upward along the pyramid: to move from lower castes to higher ones, overcoming inter-caste barriers.

Inter-caste barriers are expressed in rules invented by representatives of the higher castes to limit the inflow of representatives of the lower castes. These rules are aimed at preserving the status quo. To move upward, you need to know the rules of the caste you intend to enter. However, that is not enough. Far more important is the ability to manage people while slipping away from their attempts to manage you.

This is the main principle of growth in our world: the one who knows how to manage people and does not let them manage themselves can reach the highest level, even despite restrictive rules. Because people cancel and break the very rules they invented.

Rules prescribe acting in a way that makes it convenient for those who created them to manage you. However, if you manage those who manage the rules, you become above the rules and above those who invented them. Management is the key moment in the art of creating your own fate.

In essence, the only skill necessary to achieve success is the skill of managing people and circumstances. But, as an ancient wisdom says, if you want to learn to manage the world, first learn to manage yourself. That is not hard if you know what a human being is and how they function. Yet the overwhelming majority of people on planet Earth do not know this.

Misunderstanding the structure of the human psyche is, in essence, an internal obstacle to growth. Alongside external obstacles--inter-caste barriers--it prevents you from moving upward along the pyramid. Inside us lies the key to changing the world. Whoever understands themselves and masters the science of self-management can change themselves and transform the world around them. Changing yourself in the right way is the main and only way to get from the lower castes to the higher ones.

To begin with, it is important to understand this: our perception of the surrounding world is one hundred percent determined by the traits of our personality--by how strong or weak our Ego is, what life principles we follow, what we fear, what we fight. In essence, we do not see the surrounding world as such; we only see its subjective image created by our consciousness.

In turn, that subjective image determines all our judgments about the real world and, ultimately, our behavior. Other people can evaluate only what we do or say. It is by our words and deeds that they identify us, attributing certain qualities to what is called a human personality.

Our perception of the world and its interpretation did not arise on their own, but as a result of upbringing and acquired experience. However, experience is secondary, because we interpret it on the basis of stereotypes that were implanted in our consciousness during upbringing. A child does not know the concepts of "beauty," "ugliness," "good," "evil." Parents give names to phenomena and interpret their essence. It is parents, and then caregivers, teachers, friends, and other people around us who constantly implant stereotypical evaluations, statements, and explanations into the child's mind.

Stereotypes fill a person's consciousness from top to bottom, determining all their judgments and actions. A system of stereotypes very quickly becomes the foundation of personality--a foundation that, on autopilot, makes dozens and hundreds of different decisions for us: it chooses how to react to an event, what line to add in a conversation, what action to take. Personality is nothing more than a set of stereotypes.

Realizing this fact is important because it allows us to highlight three key points in our understanding of ourselves:
- the nature of human behavior in different situations is stereotypical;
- stereotypes are highly stable;
- we are almost unaware of them, considering them either life principles or truths that require no proof.

That is why people, as a mass, do not understand what factors actually influence their actions. That is why it is so hard for them to change their habitual way of thinking and, consequently, their lives.

At the same time, changing yourself is the main driver of personal and social growth. If you want to move forward, you must transform your personality, or, in other words, reshape the system of stereotypes that make it up. Replace unproductive stereotypical reactions, assessments, and ways of acting with productive ones.

Easy to say, hard to do. As we said above, stereotypes are most often unconscious. They are embedded too deeply in our personality for you to be able, in the moment, to identify which stereotypical structure made you speak and act this way rather than that. In addition, stereotypes are stable. They are hard to transform. Why? Because stereotypes are you.

There is nothing harder than changing yourself. It is hard to believe in your own omnipotence if you have no doubt that you are mortal, miserable, limited, cannot walk through walls, fly under the clouds, or change the world by force of thought. It is hard to allow the possibility of radical changes in your life if you consider yourself incapable of making the necessary effort. If you know for a fact which roads you can walk on and which you cannot; when you should take a step forward and when you should stay in the shadows. It is hard to be happy if you are absolutely sure that our world is disgusting and hopeless.

To part with unproductive stereotypes, you first have to identify them. Once admit to yourself that the belief "my own business is a constant headache" is nothing more than your father's verbalized fear--a father who, in his time, failed to develop his own business. And that an obsessive desire to work in government structures is the embodiment of your grandmother's dream, trembling before high ranks.

The bad news is that learning to spot your stereotypes is not so simple. It requires God-tier self-criticism. That is why, at the initial stage, we all need a Mentor--someone who will allow us to critically assess our baggage of everyday truths and life strategies and will point out which ones are worth parting with. In this sense, a book as a way of transmitting wisdom has many limitations and, of course, it cannot replace a real Mentor. But it can point to the Path on which, sooner or later, the right person will appear.

The good news is that any personality can be rebuilt. Since it is a set of stereotypes, that set can be transformed into the needed configuration depending on the desired result. Your personality is only a tool for achieving your goals. You can and should tune it so that new traits contribute in the best possible way to realizing your aspirations. And if your aspirations change, you will need to reprogram your personality again, adapting yourself to new conditions of the Game and tuning yourself for a new victory.

Functional Roles
A human personality can exist in certain stable states--roles. The term "role" has been used by many authors in various contexts, and in order not to confuse anyone, I will give a definition of what is meant by "role" within the framework of [Theory of Castes and Roles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PDHZRCC).

A functional role (hereafter simply a role) is the totality of our stereotypes of thinking and action, manifesting in how we solve problems and how we relate to people.

Like a stereotype, a role has basic properties: it is poorly accessible to self-awareness and highly stable. A role defines only those of your traits (stereotypes) that matter specifically in the context of controlling fate. We are not talking about the obvious roles of parent and child, man and woman. We are talking only about those roles that exist in the world of castes--in a world where you steadily move toward success, produce some kind of value, and receive a reward.

HIGHER ROLES
ROLE
KEY TRAITS
Leader
Generates ideas and unites people around some large-scale task, leads them forward. Manages more at the level of emotions and the heart than of common sense. Has vision, can indicate a direction of development, inspire people. Fearless, finds common ground with everyone, knows how to be liked.

Politician
Manages people within a hierarchy. Makes and maintains connections. Lobbies, threatens, seduces, achieves results. Enters strategic unions and alliances. Watches the balance of power and ensures the interests of the people and groups to which they belong. Recruits, claims supporters. Creates nothing. Knows how to select staff and manage them. Influences people, can be liked.

Financier
Manages financial flows, controls resources, is oriented toward reducing costs while increasing profit, and sees the growth of capitalization as their main task. Manages people, influences them, defends the interests of their organization.

MEDIUM ROLES
ROLE
KEY TRAITS
Producer
Looks for ideas, people, resources. Brings everything together. Ensures the realization of ideas. Creates teams and manages them. Creates, but in a project sense. The result of their activity is bringing an idea to life.

Manager
Manages people, organizes them to perform various tasks, analyzes the situation, allocates resources, controls the course of work. Motivates, rewards, punishes. Creates value through managing people. The result of their activity is delivering a project on time.

Dealmaker
Makes profitable deals, finds clients, negotiates terms. A communicator, negotiator, manipulator. Influences people, understands how to earn money. The result of their activity is a deal.

Advisor
Observes, analyzes, draws conclusions, and based on them gives advice. Outlines prospects and alternatives, brings in experts, lobbies. Understands the subject well, but produces nothing themselves. Does not manage other people, but can exert great influence. The result is a change in people's vision, their evaluation of what is happening, and their course of action.

Teacher
Teaches other people. Does not manage, but can exert minimal influence. The result is people mastering new knowledge and skills, sometimes a change of vision.

LOWER ROLES
ROLE
KEY TRAITS
Specialist
Works with their head. Implements projects, finds contractors, accepts work, analyzes, designs, plans, creates, consults. This is a scientist, analyst, accountant, editor, IT specialist, and so on. Works with non-material objects, generates ideas, solves applied tasks, answers the question "HOW" posed by managers. The result of their activity is solving a specific task.

Doer
Works with their hands, performs operations with material objects: processing material, assembling devices, packaging products, operating machines, issuing and receiving material objects, transporting something or someone, security, and so on. Does not manage people. The result of their activity is successful manipulations with material objects.

To avoid possible confusion, I propose once again clarifying the meaning of the concepts "caste" and "role." What is what.

Castes, within our theory, are a metaphorical expression of your social and financial status--an exact definition of where you are right now in the system of human hierarchy, and what you have achieved.

Roles are the set of your personal characteristics: professional skills, behavioral patterns, psychological attitudes and values.

In other words, a caste is your location on the map, and a role is the resources (equipment, physical conditioning, navigation system) that brought you to that point. A caste is a destination point; a role is a vehicle. A caste is an effect; a role is a cause.

The higher a role is in the hierarchy system, the more influence it implies over others, the fewer people play it. For that matter, the overwhelming majority of representatives of homo sapiens spend their lives either in the role of the Doer or the Specialist. And that is logical. The entire domestic education system--from kindergarten to university--is designed to prepare Doers or Specialists.

You are not taught effective communication, not taught to manage other people, resolve conflicts, and negotiate. You are taught to solve tasks in one professional field or another: to answer the question "How?" rather than "What?" and "Why?" In addition, in schools, colleges, and universities--everywhere--we are surrounded by Teachers and Specialists. And if you consider that in childhood and youth the main way of acquiring useful experience is observing older comrades, the conclusion is obvious: we enter the adult world as Doers and Specialists.

Despite such "role doom" at the beginning of human life, it is precisely role that is the key to social growth. Conscious change of role is what allows you to move upward along the caste pyramid. But before moving on to analyzing the mechanisms of growth, I suggest we take a closer look at what one role or another is. What psychological attitudes and values it projects onto its performer.

Attitudes and Values of the Higher Roles
LEADER
The main value for the Leader is the realization of their ideas and projects. They see a light ahead and lead other people toward it. Nothing exists for them besides that light and the team. Therefore, broadly speaking, they are not attached to anything. The desire to reach the goal crowds out all other feelings. Serving the beckoning light is the essence of the purpose of a person in this role. At the same time, the Leader is wise in evaluating and selecting people, because they look at them with the eyes of a seer. They understand that each new member of the team must have the right set of qualities in order to have the desire and ability to swear allegiance to the idea--to go through something like a purification, becoming part of an egregore. Of course, this is a metaphor. Its purpose is to express the sacred nature of the Leader. This nature allows them not to use orders, threats, or appeals to rules in managing team members. The Leader opens the future to people, and people rush after them.

POLITICIAN
The key value for the Politician is their influence on others. And by others we mean both those who are equal to them in status and those who are below or above. In practice, their own position is the priority. Therefore the Politician makes sophisticated efforts to hold onto it. They influence, twist, threaten, set people up, catch people out, make friends, make enemies, accumulate information and release it in measured doses. Yes, a Politician can support some project. They can raise a socially significant idea as a banner and actively lobby it in higher circles. But all projects, ideas, and initiatives are, for the Politician, nothing more than a means of holding and elevating their position. In themselves they are not part of the value list.

OFFICIAL
For the Official, the main value is good relationships with leadership. They perfectly understand the rules of the Game: if you want to stay in your place, be so kind as to protect the interests of those who put you there. The Official is concerned with preserving what they have. They prefer not to take risks if extra movement can threaten the system's stability. Choosing between opportunities and stability, they will always choose the latter--even if, as a result, their company, department, or country loses significant privileges or profit. Growth is always risk, and the Official tends to minimize any risks.

Attitudes and Values of the Medium Roles
FINANCIER
The Financier values profit, growth of capitalization, and low risk. Everything else is from the evil one. Ideas, business models, team spirit--that is lyricism. In the end it all comes down to whether profit is growing and what we are risking. This is an important role because it allows you to accumulate the fruits of the labor of people performing other roles. It is the Financier who monitors the balance of incoming and outgoing cash flows. They also set boundaries for the Entrepreneur's unrestrained creativity, cool the Manager's organizational fervor, extinguish the Dealmaker's reckless gambling fever, and block the Specialist's perfectionism. In essence, the Financier acts as a navigator in a financial ocean. It is this role that creates the monetary equivalent of the value of products and services, because value has long ceased to be mountains of gold; it is properly composed financial reports.

ENTREPRENEUR
For the Entrepreneur, the opportunity to bring their idea to life is valuable. Therefore, people who can help with that matter to them. And money, since their ideas must always generate profit. In other words, for a person in this role the main thing is finding and realizing ideas for enrichment. The Entrepreneur is not attached to a specific field of activity. They are ready to earn money on agricultural machinery or on the production of toys--as long as it makes sense. By comparison, the Specialist cannot imagine their life without the profession in which they have succeeded. By the way, neither can the Manager, who cannot imagine themselves outside the management system of which they are a part. The Entrepreneur is free of such frameworks. They are oriented only toward results.

MANAGER
The main value for the Manager is achieving a result through the correct organization of resources. They view the people around them as elements of a mechanism--ideally, a perfectly tuned one. Subordinates must be motivated, responsibilities delegated, timelines controlled, tasks executed. Since human resources are the main thing that makes the mechanism work, the Manager is oriented toward people. It is important for them to understand people: to know which lever to press in order to make someone act in the right way. To understand how to build relationships so the mechanism does not fail.

DEALMAKER
The main value for the Dealmaker is deal margin. Therefore they strive to close the maximum number of deals with maximum benefit. They have no attachment to what they sell. Knowledge of the product is only a way to persuade a buyer or a seller to agree to better terms. The Dealmaker does not focus on problems and solutions. Their attention is focused on the opportunity to find something that sells well and sell it. There is no point in proposing to the Dealmaker that they modernize or optimize something, train someone, bring an extraordinary idea to life, and so on. They do not see value in actions that do not lead them to their main goal: obtaining more money.

ADVISOR
The Advisor values the attention of the powerful. If the Teacher usually teaches those who are younger or lower in status, the Advisor prefers to advise those who are far above. It is important for them to feel needed by people with power and money. The Advisor knows the price of money, but social status will always come first. It matters to them that the person they advise recognizes their uniqueness, the power of their intellect, the significance of their experience, and treats them accordingly. At the center is the ritualization of the relationship; otherwise the Advisor turns into one of countless faceless experts. This is what distinguishes the Advisor role from that of the Teacher or the Specialist. Offering the Advisor to solve a problem or teach someone is useless; they will not respond.

TEACHER
The Teacher loves to teach, explain, help. They derive satisfaction from the process of transmitting knowledge. They like watching how, thanks to their efforts, the student's personality is transformed. This fills the Teacher's life with meaning--a meaning that is more important than money and social status. Although status matters too. The Teacher feels like the center of the student or school world. Without admitting it to themselves, they enjoy their power; they take pleasure in being listened to, being obeyed, having their opinion recognized as true and hardly ever criticized. Because the Teacher is constantly surrounded by students, they become accustomed to feeling like the smartest and most experienced one. It is extremely unpleasant for them to leave this comfort zone. If the Teacher is offered a task that is new to them, they will fall into doubt and almost certainly refuse.
