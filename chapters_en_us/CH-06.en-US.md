# CH-06 -- ATTITUDES AND VALUES OF THE LOWER ROLES

Attitudes and Values of the Lower Roles
SPECIALIST
The Specialist feels attached to their professional field. When they do their work, they get real pleasure from it. At the same time, it is important that their labor be paid decently. They see this as one confirmation of their professional significance. If a self-respecting Specialist is not satisfied with their salary, they change organizations, but not their professional sphere. The profession is the foundation of the Specialist's identity. Another global value for a person in this role is their reputation in the industry community. Being evaluated by peers or higher-ranking colleagues as a strong professional is one of the conditions for overall life satisfaction.
The Specialist is ready to take on solving new, nonstandard tasks, but only if they fall within their professional field. Beyond it, they prefer not to step. Their growth zone is the uncovering of professional secrets, ever deeper mastery of their craft. That is the meaning of their life; that is the source of their self-respect and pride.
DOER
For the Doer, the process and the result of labor are valuable. The higher the quality and volume of work, the higher the level of satisfaction with what they do. Naturally, on the condition that pay grows along with output. Any initiative by the Doer aimed at increasing performance metrics should be encouraged. This is the foundation of their worldview. If you offer the Doer a job they are unfamiliar with, you will almost certainly get a refusal. At the same time, they will gladly take on a familiar task if the terms suit them.
Extremely rarely does a person exist within the confines of only one role. For example, a Manager, especially one who is still getting used to a new status, may from time to time become a Specialist. An Advisor sometimes takes on the role of Teacher. An Entrepreneur--that of Financier. People are multifaceted creatures and prefer to live several lives at once. However, each of us has a dominant role, and it is that role that determines our relationships with people like us and, at the same time, the trajectory of social growth.
The Importance of Roles for Growth
Based on the caste metaphor, the growth strategy is movement from lower castes to higher ones. The defining feature of the former is dependence of social and financial status on direct work: how much you worked is how much you get to eat. The defining feature of the latter is the presence of a system that, regardless of the time and effort spent maintaining it, generates income.
If we talk about a growth strategy, it is set by a simple sequence. First you increase your income enough to be able to set aside half into a reserve fund. Next you become independent of employers--the options can be different: you start investing, consulting, earning on online teaching, selling on marketplaces, writing in-demand books. Then you create a system where other people work for you, multiplying your income and freeing you from direct work. You are left with the functions of control and development. The specific strategy depends on what caste you are in and which roles dominate in your personality.
A key premise of [Theory of Castes and Roles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PDHZRCC) says that moving up the hierarchy is based on changing roles from less effective to more effective ones. Less effective roles are those that have no potential to increase income and free you up for development. More effective ones, on the contrary, imply that potential.
The Doer role is among the least effective. A person in this role has virtually no opportunity to increase income and raise social status. It is typical of laborers, salespeople, service staff in various industries, junior medical staff, and so on. I will not go on about what social position is occupied by those for whom this role is dominant. I am sure that among my readers there are not many who have devoted their life to it.
Let's make this clear: a role is not the equivalent of your current job or position. A role is the set of dominant stereotypes that determine your system of values, priorities, and your attitude toward people and situations. If your dominant role is Doer and you have no intention of parting with it, then your chances for growth are virtually zero. Because your work is built on the art of handling objects, not people. This means your social status and income will always be limited by your working hours and physical stamina. The exception is jewelers and those who create truly unique expensive things. But such professionals go their own way, which we will talk about later--there is no point in discussing an exception in the context of a general rule.
In all other cases, the Doer role is a social dead end. It gives you no way to move from the lower castes to the higher ones.
In developing countries, especially where agricultural production is the main occupation, the overwhelming number of people belong to the caste of Slaves and have the dominant role of Doer. In more or less developed industrial and post-industrial countries, most people play the role of Specialist and Teacher. This is natural: the economy requires a huge number of qualified personnel in the intellectual sphere, so professional knowledge and skills are in active demand. Hence the number of all kinds of teachers, trainers, coaches, consultants. Today, even to solve the most unusual problem you can find the right specialist!
Since we live in a world oversaturated with information, society has an acute need for those who help navigate its boundless sea. These are various kinds of intermediaries, agents, legal specialists, consultants, sales managers, and so on. All of them are in the Specialist role, less often Teacher. They work in private and government companies. They research, invent, search, create, analyze, calculate, keep records, teach others. People in these roles do millions of intellectual things connected with millions of abstract categories, but as a rule they do nothing that implies managing people. And if so, they have no chance to seriously advance toward the higher castes, because, I remind you, we live in a world of people, and your advancement depends on how you build relationships with them, not on how you handle material or abstract objects.
At this line I get asked several typical questions, so I will answer them in advance.
Why do representatives of these roles work only as employees? Because building your own thing requires completely different personal qualities, which this role lacks.
Why do those for whom these roles dominate have no chance of growth? Because growth implies a rise in social status or a noticeable increase in income--a move into the caste of Professionals, Free Agents, or Entrepreneurs. And that, as I said above, implies the presence of qualities that the Specialist and Teacher simply do not have.
If you get hired somewhere, you become part of a system in which your position is determined by two parameters: your professional specialty and your job title. Specialty sets the industry and the range of issues you'll have to work with. Job title is your place in the hierarchy. It shows whom you report to, who reports to you, as well as the scope of your rights, duties, authority, and privileges.
The higher the status, the higher the income and the higher the caste you belong to, even if you work for hire. Working for someone else, you can have three different statuses that correspond to three different castes. Here are the possible options:
- be in the caste of Slaves, while being in the role of Doer, Specialist, Teacher, Dealmaker, Manager;
- be in the caste of Professionals, while being in the role of Specialist, Dealmaker, Financier, Politician;
- be in the caste of Top Managers or Siloviks, while being in the role of Entrepreneur, Financier, Manager, Politician.
Why the Specialist and Teacher Roles Offer No Chance of Growth
As I have already said, these roles do not provide for a rise in social status or level of prosperity. If they dominate in a person's personality, then their value system will be tied to professional development, not career growth.
Professional skills are needed to solve applied tasks well, but they are not enough for moving from caste to caste. The thing is, climbing the social ladder depends on other people--your boss, your boss's boss, deal participants, and so on. Whether to pay you more money, whether to promote you--the answers to these questions are only partly tied to your professional skills. Of course, it is assumed that you are a qualified specialist who handles your responsibilities. But for that you get a salary, and everything beyond that--sorry--is determined by your competencies in a different area.
The key role is played by how you influence people and build relationships with them. Or, in other words, whether you have the skill of management, at least in embryonic form. Let's be honest: good Specialists and Teachers can boast of it extremely rarely. Simply because it fits poorly into the value system of these roles, which is based on devotion to one's profession.
Specialists and Teachers do not understand the mechanism of social growth. They do not understand that any reward in the form of a raise or a title is an act of evaluation. You are being evaluated. And you are being evaluated by people. And people are prone to subjectivity. They are far more willing to reward someone who is pleasant to them, convenient, useful, than someone who virtuously performs their job duties. They will forgive you incompetence, but they will not forgive a lack of loyalty. Likewise, they will not forgive an inability to fit into the expectations of the decision-maker about you. Because someone expects compliance, someone healthy ambition, someone a willingness to keep them company at the bathhouse. And everyone together expects that you will be able to build the most productive relationships with them.
Just imagine this situation: you work in a department with ten other specialists just like you. Everyone handles their duties about the same, everyone is the same age. Question: which of you will be appointed to the position of the manager being promoted? Yes, you are right. The one who had the best relationship with them. Remember: if you are on good terms with someone, they will forgive you many mistakes; if you are on bad terms, even your obvious achievements will not help you.
A Specialist and a Teacher who successfully handle applied tasks are often unable to build productive relationships with leadership and colleagues, because their value system implies professional competition. Moreover, strong professionals for whom these roles dominate are sometimes prone to arrogance. They see that their boss is less qualified than they are and draw the wrong conclusions. Namely: the boss is an idiot, I'm better than him, it's unfair that he's ordering me around! Such reasoning invisibly affects the relationship with the boss. The boss feels uncomfortable next to an arrogant subordinate and, lacking in many cases objective ways to assess the employee's effectiveness, resorts to subjective ones. That is, they tell themselves: I don't like him, he's not my person, I don't want to help him grow. In essence, this effectively puts an end to the subordinate's career.
Problems also arise when it comes to negotiating a raise. Since for both the Specialist and the Teacher the first priority is always the ability to do their work, they subconsciously fear losing it. This fear keeps them from going all the way--raising the bar, stating their desires, making the right proposals, maneuvering, negotiating. Because of this, in the labor market in the Specialist niche, the employer sets the terms, not the applicant. As a result, good pros sit for years, if not decades, on a rate that was set once long ago, refusing decisive action.
What can help the Specialist and Teacher? Master, alongside their habitual roles, one more--the Dealmaker. It is precisely the Dealmaker's skills that will make the Specialist and Teacher roles more balanced. They will give their holder a chance at social growth.
The Dealmaker is aimed at closing the most advantageous deals possible. They know how to sell above the market, they are resistant to pressure and are not afraid to set conditions. This applies not only to selling an abstract product, but to selling oneself. That is why the Dealmaker role is salvation for Specialists and Teachers.
By the way, many people confuse the Specialist role with the Professional caste. I constantly hear: "You place me in the caste of Slaves, but I'm an excellent specialist!" The thing is that a Professional, unlike a Specialist, is a person who combines in themselves the roles of both Specialist and Dealmaker. They not only possess a craft and have a professional reputation, but also know how to properly present their capabilities. They have a built-in sense of their own uniqueness, so the Professional has no problem demanding a high salary from an employer. They simply know they are worth it and will easily sell themselves--if not to one company, then to another.
When we get to this point, I often hear the question: "How?" How do you bargain for high pay for your work? For most Specialists and Teachers, the advice "Become a Dealmaker" sounds like an anecdote: "Mice, mice, become hedgehogs!" Great idea, but you'd like detailed instructions.
What throws people off most is the negative baggage of their own screwups. Any pro, no matter how cool a specialist they are, makes mistakes from time to time. That's normal--we're all human. But for a person whose dominant roles are Specialist and Teacher, it is extremely hard, for the duration of negotiations, to forget their own imperfection. It is enough to remind them of a couple of missteps a year or two ago, and all their fire to "talk salary" disappears somewhere. Experienced Managers know this perfectly well and skillfully use their subordinates' weak spots.
What to do?
First, separate the past from the present and the future. If you come to talk about a raise, that is the zone of the present and the future, not the past. You are bargaining here and now. The person who once made mistakes no longer exists; now in their place is a completely different person. And that person deserves a raise. No communication tricks are needed here. In most cases it is enough to believe yourself that this is true. Close the topic of the past and talk about the future.
Second, understand that your negotiation counterpart is under the power of two emotional vectors. The first is connected with risks: fear of mistakes, anxiety, suspicions, paranoia. The second--with opportunities: vanity, greed, avarice. I always recommend to my subscribers and clients, when it comes to money (salary, fee, a percentage on a deal), to bet on the emotions of the second vector. Show your opponent an enticing opportunity and give their greed, avarice, and vanity time to deal with their fears. Make their consciousness justify you. Move negotiations from the plane of reason to the plane of emotion. Give your partner time for the emotions of the second vector to mature and grow strong. And then they will come to you themselves. And you don't rush; even hold the process back a little so the tension doesn't weaken. Break the path to the goal into stages and implement them gradually, presenting each as another success and another step closer to the goal. That will motivate your opponent and keep them from burning out. People always willingly pay for stages, because it creates an illusion of control, success, and risk minimization.
