# CH-09 -- THE TRAP OF THE MYTH OF YOUR DREAM JOB

The Trap of the Myth of Your Dream Job
As we have already found out, the value system instilled during growing up sets your dominant role. That role, in turn, determines your desires. You want from life what the dominant role implies. And that is where contradictions arise--contradictions that lead to fatal results.
On the one hand, our desires are more than concrete. We strive to get good housing, expensive things that will provide a high standard of living, big money to help loved ones, and so on. On the other hand, Specialists and the Teachers who have joined them quite consciously declare a desire to engage in professional activity. From their point of view, it is precisely this that brings them real pleasure. Unfortunately, a dream job very often does not imply even a tenth of the necessary income. As a result, the two desires begin to contradict each other.
I want to be a journalist and I want to live in a mansion; I want to teach kids and I want to fly to the Maldives five times a year; I want to design buildings and I want to have so much money that I can provide for my whole family for decades ahead. Let's be honest: being simply a Specialist or a Teacher is not enough if you want to consider yourself truly successful and your life filled with meaning.
When a person is young, they do not think about this. The insight comes around thirty-five. At that age, it is time to sum up the stretch of life you have lived, and a feeling appears: "something is off, I've achieved too little, something is missing." In other words, a person begins to experience frustration. And the reason is obvious: the meaning of life is distorted under the influence of the dominant role.
It seems to Specialists, Teachers, even partly to Dealmakers, that the meaning of their life is doing what they love. Is that true? No. As I wrote earlier, the meaning of life is the emotions connected with evaluating the prospects of your activity, with faith that it will bring you the values you want. If what you love does not lead to real results, it gradually loses meaning.
At the same time, the profession can absolutely provide immediate satisfaction. You give a great lecture--you get a portion of gratitude from students; you write a powerful article--you earn readers' praise. But globally nothing changes in your life: you do not move up the hierarchy, you do not overcome inter-caste barriers. Let's not fool ourselves: a person who makes their favorite work the meaning of their existence lives only for today. They focus on short-term desires and ignore the long view. And what is that if not banal immaturity?
People come to me and ask: "What should I do? I want to be successful: have money and do what I love, but I can't make it work." Naturally, because many are focused only on pleasure from the activity itself. But they need to widen their focus to include what actually produces results.
It is useless to tell such a person: "What you're doing is, from the point of view of your life path, a waste of time." They will not hear you, because such statements contradict the very essence of their existence and call their basic values into question. However, when you suggest they formulate their desires as concrete material and non-material goods, the person begins to understand: a profession is only a tool. We pick up a drill to hang a shelf and get rid of chaos in the house, not for the process of making holes in the wall.
Your task is to add to your professional activity role functions--purposeful efforts that lead to development. Note: I am not calling on you to stop doing what you love; I am suggesting you understand how that thing can lead you into a higher caste. The world of castes does not care what you like and what you don't. That's your personal business. To succeed in it, you must find a balance between what gives you satisfaction and what can bring results.
Let's run an experiment. I don't know you--those who read this book--but I will assume you belong to the category of people for whom, in the modern world, many doors are open. You are smart, creative, talented, ambitious, and you want career growth. Most likely you love your work or, at least, you have something you genuinely enjoy doing. Attention, question: which of the professional fates described below would suit you?
In the first option, you are a young architect, or designer, or business consultant, or trader, or advertising and PR specialist, or programmer, or stylist, or marketer--a representative of any intellectual profession. You worked hard, entered every competition, won prizes. You got hired at a well-known company, advanced thanks to talent and the ability to build relationships. They made you a partner, and then you created your own company. You hired young ambitious employees, won several prestigious tenders, built a serious client base, gradually expanded your lineup of products or services, became a cult figure in your industry. You are successful, well-off, fully self-realized, you write books, run seminars.
In the second option, you are the same talented young specialist in any profession. You worked hard, entered every competition, won prizes. You got a job at a well-known firm, worked yourself to the bone, but you never got promoted. They constantly dumped difficult clients on you and shoved you into disadvantageous situations. Your buddy was made a partner, and then he created his own company, hired people, became a cult figure. And you kept working first in one company, then in another. Money was always tight, prospects were zero. You celebrated your forty-fifth birthday with a mortgage debt, chronic depression, and stage-two alcoholism.
So which option would you choose for yourself? I suspect the first. And it's not about whether you want to start your own business or become someone's partner right now. The point is that when we look back to evaluate the path we've traveled, we always look at results. We and those around us are interested in them, and only them.
At the same time, you need to understand: success can be achieved in any profession. The only thing that matters is what roles you take on, what goals you set, what path you choose. The question "Do you like your profession?" is equivalent to the question "Do you like the experience it gives you?" We answer "YES" as long as the activity brings satisfaction, there is novelty, an ability to see results of your work and develop. We answer "NO" if there is no development, results are not visible or do not please you, and you do not get pleasure from your labor.
A person wastes a lot of time because of the myth of the work of their life. First they search for it for a long time, then they seem to find it and convince everyone around them that only this profession can make them happy. However, in most cases, sooner or later disappointment comes. The person plunges into the depths of a personal crisis, only to spend months, if not years, mastering a new profession. Then comes euphoria from the feeling "I found myself," then again--disappointment and a personal crisis. This cycle can repeat until old age. And only much later, the particularly clear-sighted understand: it's not about the profession, it's about concrete results. Any activity can acquire the status of "dream job" if it brings you money, career growth, great projects, and in the end recognition. Most, however, retire still under the power of the myth of a dream job, and then, in a surge of self-flagellation, complain to their grandchildren and children: "I just never got lucky enough to find myself."
Successful and Unsuccessful Fates
So, any activity can create room both for rapid growth and for endless stagnation. And the profession has no meaning whatsoever here. As I said earlier, you can succeed in any field.
It is important to understand: the higher you rise in the hierarchy, the less you depend on professional skills, and the more you depend on personal qualities that manifest in you through one role or another. This is an important thesis, because it helps answer the question: "What should you learn in order to move forward?" [Theory of Castes and Roles](https://www.amazon.com/dp/B07PDHZRCC) gives an exhaustive answer to it, which we will formulate a little later.
For now, we need to understand this: there are only two types of fates--successful and unsuccessful. For your fate to belong to the first type, you need to:
- keep in focus a goal that sets the vector of your movement for many years ahead, while harmoniously fitting into your value system and not contradicting life principles;
- persistently overcome obstacles on the way to that goal, simultaneously strengthening your own will;
- constantly stoke your faith in the attainability of the goal, not lose motivation;
- plan your efforts so that even intermediate stages of movement toward the goal give visible results--promotions, wins in competitions, financial metrics, high-profile projects, and so on.
Thus, we have come to an understanding of the basic technology that lies at the foundation of any successful fate. This technology consists of the following elements:
- the right trajectory on the map of the world of castes and roles;
- the presence of an intermediate goal connected not with answering the question "what should I do?" but with acquiring the skills and qualities of the role you need;
- clarifying values, life principles, and desires;
- finding the meaning of life--faith in the correctness and effectiveness of your efforts;
- external help in the form of mentoring for the right choice of goal and support along the way.
Each role has its own problems; each caste has its own laws. All this generates a mass of non-obvious obstacles that people run into on the way to their goal and cannot move forward. They mark time, walk in circles, roll back, and sincerely do not understand what is happening.
In such cases I am guided by a simple thesis: a person is the cause of all events in their life. Not always, but as a rule, they themselves create the circumstances that lead them into a dead end. And they themselves turn out to be unable to see a way out of it. For example, anyone can be laid off, become a victim of an economic crisis, or run into partners who are not clean-handed. But someone ends their career there, forever resigning themselves to existence in the lower castes. And someone takes closeness to the bottom as an excellent reason to push off from it and reach a new, much higher, level.
Often people hit a dead end simply because they do not know the basic rules and laws of the world of castes and roles. Once I discovered this, I devoted a lot of time to analyzing typical dead ends and traps on the life path. It was important for me to understand what the main reasons are that make a person get stuck in the swamp of a meaningless life. Or seemingly meaningful, but unproductive. Working with such "stuck" clients, time after time I came to the conclusion: any dead end has a mental nature at its base. A person stops growing because they stop seeing the goal. They either have no idea where to go and what to apply effort to, or they are under the power of false goals. And here it is important to give them Vision--to help them gain the ability to build their life path.
That is, we are dealing with an expansion of the concept of "role" into the concept of "path." A path is a series of roles that help us move toward our goals, and the totality of paths is fate. Thus, if we deepen the thoughts expressed earlier in this book, you should not choose a role (not a title, not a set of skills, not a collection of approaches to solving tasks), but the process of changing roles. In essence, speaking about the formation of a successful fate, we must speak about choosing a journey, choosing a road that runs through an entire life.
By making the creation of our fate conscious, we are rebuilding our own "I." What is fate? It is a change of roles and, as a result, a change of castes. A person masters new kinds of activity, solves tasks, deals with arising problems, and along the way transforms their personality. Changes in the inner and outer worlds go in parallel. These processes are inseparably linked. One of the myths of our reality is that before you change your life, you must change yourself first. For example, you want a promotion--first become confident. Alas, that is not how it works. We gain confidence only when we achieve something significant, when we have a reason for pride.
True changes of our "I" happen only in response to changes in the outer world, and changes in the outer world are the result of an impulse coming from within. In other words, working on yourself and creating your fate is a single process that cannot be divided into internal and external stages. There is no way to grow personally without achieving real results. But it is not only concrete achievements that force us to change--new positions, money hitting the account, or public recognition. We change when we meet obstacles on our path. They provoke will within us, release hidden strength, become a reason for a cleansing transformation of personality.
The key stage of development is understanding that your line of work does not matter. It does not matter whether you sell real estate, write books, or trade trinkets on marketplaces. What matters is that you walk the path you chose, overcoming obstacles, showing willpower, and thereby freeing your spirit. An understanding comes to you: any action in an attempt to achieve a result is nothing more than an instrument for transforming yourself and the world. And the surrounding reality is a school for God's children. And if you learn to act effectively in a world with the laws described here, your opportunities will become limitless.
Analysis of Life Trajectories: How Key Roles Shape a Successful Fate
To make the metaphor of castes and roles as clear as possible, I suggest we model several typical fates. We will call them life trajectories, because this phrase reflects as accurately as possible the fact that human life is a route laid out on the map of the world of castes and roles.
TRAJECTORY 1.
A successful person, often not burdened by excessive erudition, who rose to the upper caste--Oligarchs, Top Managers, or even Rulers. Let's call this trajectory "Successful Businessman."
TRAJECTORY 2.
A more or less successful middle-aged person, very hardworking, energetic, has created and is developing their business, as a rule a small one. Let's call their life trajectory "Active Entrepreneur."
TRAJECTORY 3.
A talented, successful person who managed to realize their creative ambitions and reached the upper caste of Businessmen while remaining a creative personality. Let's call them "Successful Creator."
TRAJECTORY 4.
A talented but unsuccessful person. They tried many kinds of activity, showed decent results everywhere, but failed to make them systematic. In the end they abandoned what they were doing and moved to another field. By the end of life they ended up with nothing. Let's call their trajectory "Talented Loser."
TRAJECTORY 5.
A person who worked diligently all their life but did not reach the upper castes and in retirement ended up practically broke. Let's call them "Wretched Workhorse."
Starting conditions:
- Successful Businessman: Childhood in a small town. Poor family, parents worked all the time, constant need. Barely finished school, got into college. Never counted on parents' help. While still in college, started a small business; money appeared. Pragmatic, brash, smart. Always made connections quickly. Paid attention to wealthy people and wondered: where did they get their money? Interests are concentrated in the material sphere.
- Active Entrepreneur: Childhood in a military town. Father in the military. Whole life--from garrison to garrison. Finished school, ten grades. Applied to a military academy. Failed the exam. Served in the army.
- Successful Creator: Born and raised in the capital in the family of a famous musician. Spent all childhood in comfort, pursuing their hobby. Decided to become a director. With the father's help, got into an arts university.
- Talented Loser: Born into an intelligentsia family--an industry scientist and a teacher. Comfortable childhood. Talented, smart. Learned everything quickly and effortlessly. Did different kinds of creative work. Parents said: "The main thing is that it's interesting to work." Never thought about putting bread on the table. Went to a technical university in a specialty that seemed interesting.
- Wretched Workhorse: Born in the capital in the family of a teacher and a doctor. The family lived modestly, but close-knit. The main value is labor and labor honor. Did not shine with achievements, but pulled through with persistence, without imagination. After school went to a technical college.
